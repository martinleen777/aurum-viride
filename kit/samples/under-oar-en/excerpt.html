CHAPTER ONE

Schoolboys on the Oder

An eight-oar from Ohlau to Stettin and the sea, 1881 — Wassersport, 1883

In the summer of 1881 a schoolmaster in the small Silesian town of Ohlau took ten of his senior pupils, his own son among them, down the Oder in an eight-oared gig to Stettin, some 450 kilometres of river, and then out on to the Baltic. Rowing was barely known in German schools; the parents were warned on all sides that the boys would collapse after a few days and come home by train. They did not. Dr C. Lampe wrote the voyage up for the Schlesische Zeitung and, two years later, at greater length for Wassersport, in the hope of persuading other headmasters to let their boys loose on the rivers.

His account is the oldest long river journey in the journal and sets the tone for many that followed: the boat, the kit and the rations described with care; a strict little discipline on board; and a teacher's delight in the towns, battlefields and poets along the way. A second Ohlau voyage over the same water, made three years later, follows in the next chapter. The section headings are ours. — L. Dudakov

Dr C. Lampe, senior master at the Ohlau Gymnasium

I. The Ohlau Gymnasium Rowing Club

I have ventured to lay the following account of a river journey through Middle and Lower Silesia, Brandenburg and Pomerania before the readers of Wassersport in the confidence that among them there will be no lack of energetic men who think the idea of opening the sport of rowing to the older boys of our grammar schools worth their notice and their support. If anywhere, it was among the friends of German rowing that I could hope to find allies in spreading it in our gymnasia and modern schools. I was thinking particularly of those gentlemen who, as members of state or municipal bodies, have some connection with our system of education. Should the description of my voyage awaken the wish to see such undertakings become common in our higher schools, it will have served its essential purpose.

In the spring of 1880 I founded a rowing club for the upper forms of our gymnasium. Within a short time it had more than thirty members, and the nearby Oder soon showed a lively traffic. To the practice of swimming was added the new and still unfamiliar spectacle of rowing. After a four-oared gig, which we called Wega, we had a handsome eight built, and we set the three stars of Orion’s belt in her flag and the name of that fine constellation on her bow. The classical superstition of the senior boys later disapproved of this, for whenever Orion took part in a long trip there was seldom any lack of storm and foul weather, the works of the ancient nimbosus Orion. Towards the end of the summer the two gigs were joined by a pair-oared outrigger, the Blitz, the gift of a senior boy named Kohlisch, who then left to serve his year in the Uhlans. Members owned eight Greenlanders privately. The first boat of this kind was brought here by V. Szarbinowski, now a lieutenant in the 64th Infantry Regiment, Prince Frederick Charles. He had probably paddled one about the Oder at Breslau as a boy, and nothing was further from my mind than the thought that at my age and at a weight of 174 pounds I could still learn to handle so small a boat safely; but it went better than I had expected, and I do not regret having had one built for myself.

When the high water of 1880 came down, a rumour went round the town: “Well, one of the rowing club has drowned already!” Many a superior wisdom heard this with the satisfaction that only the awareness of having foreseen a misfortune clearly and certainly can give. The fact, however, was that we had saved a life. A young chemist had boasted that he would swim across the swollen Oder. In the middle of the river his strength failed him; perhaps, too, this otherwise brave and nimble gymnast was a stranger to the state into which the close sight of a broad mass of water rushing past can throw a man, and which might be called horizontal giddiness. At the endangered swimmer’s cry for help our Wega, which happened to be returning from a practice row, hurried up and brought him ashore. Later, when our daredevil had recovered his former humour, he delighted me with the cheeky gasconade that it was not the Wega that had saved him, but he who had saved the Wega.
