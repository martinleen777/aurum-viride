Preface

Every week from 1883 to 1895 the Berlin journal Wassersport printed race results, club news and arguments about rules. Every summer and autumn, among these, it printed letters and serials from readers who had simply gone somewhere by boat. A schoolmaster took his pupils down the Oder in an eight. A student paddled from the source of the Danube home to Berlin. Clerks and engineers rowed their racing boats to the Baltic for a holiday. A doctor took his wife and a heavy friend down the Weser in three second-hand English canoes. These accounts were written by amateurs for other amateurs, usually within a few weeks of coming home. They are the most personal pages the journal ever printed, and this book collects them.

This first volume contains the voyages made under oar and paddle: on the rivers, lakes and canals of Germany and, in two cases, beyond. The second volume, Under Sail, contains the voyages made under sail and at sea. The chapters follow the order of the voyages themselves, from 1881 to 1893. Two accounts from abroad come next, and the book closes with the longest and youngest voyage of all, a schoolboys’ eight that rowed round two provinces of Prussia in 1895. Where several short accounts belong together — the Spreewald, the Rhine, a single Berlin season — they share a chapter.

The choice has been strict. Only accounts in which something actually happens, and which tell it well, have been included. Many more were printed: lists of stations and times, polite thanks to hospitable clubs, and translations from English magazines. The texts chosen are given in full, apart from a few digressions. These are mostly days spent ashore: a walk in the Eifel, sightseeing and hospitality in the towns along the way, and a tourist's journey to the North Cape. Each such omission is marked by a bracketed note where it occurs. Some serials were never finished, or survive only in part in the copies of the journal that have come down to us. Where an instalment is missing, the chapter says so; nothing has been supplied in its place.

The writers were oarsmen and canoeists, not authors, and their German is often hurried and sometimes careless. I have kept their tone, their jokes and their names for places and boats. Where the scan of a page could not be read with certainty it was checked against the page image, and where a writer was plainly wrong about a fact a footnote says so. The scenery they describe has mostly changed past recognition: the rivers are canalised, the weirs rebuilt, the ferry houses and the inns gone. What has not changed is the experience of these pages: a wet seat, a head wind on a long reach, an unexpected kindness at a strange landing stage, and the lights of a town coming into view after dark.

— L. Dudakov
