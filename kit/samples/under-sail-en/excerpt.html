CHAPTER ONE

Out of Vegesack

The Flying Cloud to Kiel and back, and the cutter Tell at Heligoland — Wassersport, 1883

Vegesack, on the lower Weser below Bremen, was a town of shipbuilders and pilots, and its Regatta Club was one of the few in Germany whose members took their boats to sea for pleasure. In the first numbers of Wassersport, in January and February 1883, one of them, Otto Krudewolf, described two of these annual sea trips. Both sailed with the same pilot, J. D. Luth of Geestemünde, a stout man who could not abide water as a drink.

The first account is a ship’s log, kept in an eight-metre open-sterned boat rigged like a French lugger, from the Weser across the mouth of the Elbe, up the Eider and through the old Eider canal to Kiel, and back by way of Heligoland. The second describes a cruise in the cutter Tell in August 1882. Its passengers went ashore at Heligoland for the night in a rising gale, and woke to find their ship gone. What had happened to her in the meantime was told afterwards by the pilot, and Krudewolf gives it in his words. The tone of the two accounts could hardly be more different; between them they show the whole range of North Sea yachting in the years before the Kiel Canal. Wind forces are given, as in the originals, on the twelve-point scale used at sea. — L. Dudakov

The log of the Flying Cloud

Otto Krudewolf, Bremen

In the summer of 1882 we chose the Flying Cloud for a voyage from Vegesack to Kiel. She is a boat of 8 metres length and 2.30 metres beam, carries only about three hundredweight of ballast, and draws about 50 centimetres including the keel. She is decked forward, has amidships a cabin as comfortable as the space allows, and aft is quite open like an ordinary boat, without even a washboard. She is a good sailer, very comfortable and easy to handle, and — the main thing — seaworthy.

Her build and rig resemble those of a French lugger, of the kind used at Bremerhaven by all the ship-brokers and ship-chandlers to sail out to the mouth of the Weser and “capture” for their business the sea-going ships arriving there for Bremen; for which reason they are called “privateers” here. The crew at the start of the voyage consisted of the owner, Mr James B. Boyes of Farge, the writer, the pilot J. D. Luth of Geestemünde, and a ship’s boy.

The voyage, although not always favoured by good weather, passed without any accident and without adventures of general interest. I will therefore confine myself to giving an extract from our ship’s log.

Thursday, 1 July. Morning. Ship ready to sail. 11 a.m. all hands on board. Set all sail and left the anchorage amid thundering hurrahs and a thousand good wishes from our friends. Wind WNW 4, some rain. Barometer rising. Passed Brake 1 p.m., Nordenhamm 2.20 p.m. At 3.15 p.m. reached Bremerhaven and laid the boat in the Geeste.

Friday, 2 July. Sailed out of the Geeste 9 a.m. Wind SW 4, cloudy. Barometer rising. At 10 the wind backed to NNW; beat through the Dwarsgat and anchored at the Weser lighthouse at 1 p.m.

Saturday, 3 July. 5 a.m. Wind south 3, fair, flood. Sailed for the sea; passed the Bremen lightship at 6.30, then steered N ½ E; passed double buoy D at 8 a.m., bearing SE by S two cables off. At 9 a.m. caught several fish with the mackerel line, which, fried, served us for dinner. At 9.45 passed the Western Till buoy. Wind SSE 3, glorious weather. Sea somewhat lively. Course NE. At 11.30 a.m. the landfall buoy of the Süder Piep bore SE by E, distance two sea miles. Now steered NE by E. At 12, at dinner, sighted Heligoland, bearing NW by W; depth of water 15 metres. At 2 p.m. made out the Eider lightship bearing NE by E, five sea miles off. Then a dead calm set in, which we used to take a bathe in the glorious blue waves of the North Sea, as refreshing as it was interesting. 4 p.m. wind ESE 2. As our course was now east, we had to beat. At 6 p.m. the Eider lightship bore WNW ½ W, the landfall buoy of the Eider E ½ S, in 10 metres of water. At 7 we passed the latter. Wind SE by E 4. At 8.30 reached the Eider pilot galliot, and at 10 anchored in four metres of water behind the Fliegen-Plate in the Eider.
